# Market and business analysis

*Directional estimates built from public market data and named comps, not a forecast. Not financial or investment advice.*

## The audience, sized in dollars

There are 24.8 million Asians in the United States as of 2023, making up 7% of the population, having grown 109% since 2000 — the fastest-growing major demographic group in the country (U.S. Census Bureau / Pew Research). Restricting to adult men (roughly half the population, ~78% adult) puts the core U.S. target audience at roughly **9-10 million adult Asian American men** — and that's the U.S. alone, before adding Canada, the UK, and the European cities already in the seed data (Paris, Lisbon, Berlin).

That population carries real spending weight. Asian American and Pacific Islander spending power topped $1 trillion in 2018 and was projected to break $1.3 trillion by 2023 (Nielsen), and more recent estimates put it as high as $1.6 trillion by 2024, with Asian American households spending 11% more annually on retail than the average U.S. household (Claritas). Median Asian American household income runs meaningfully above the national average.

More specifically useful than the raw dollar figure: this audience behaves in exactly the way this product is betting on. A Nielsen study found 29% of Asian Americans "prefer to buy things my friends and neighbors would approve of," versus 15% for the average U.S. consumer, and 32% share opinions by posting reviews and ratings online. That's not a generic community — it's a demographic that already over-indexes on exactly the vouching/recommending behavior this app is built around.

Streetwear and hypebeast culture reinforce the same pattern from the aspirational-spend side: 18% of Asian respondents report spending $500-1,000/month on streetwear, roughly double the 9% rate among North American and European respondents (Hypebeast/Strategy& streetwear report). That's real evidence of willingness to pay a premium for something identity-affirming and well-branded, not just functional.

### Haircut and grooming spend specifically

Estimates vary a lot by what's counted:
- Pure haircut spend: ~$158.90/year for the average American man (industry surveys).
- Broader grooming spend (products included): ~$592/year (Advanced Dermatology survey).
- Haircuts including tips: $400-700/year cited by some barbershop industry sources.

Given this audience specifically self-selects as caring more about their hair than average, it's reasonable to anchor toward the higher end of these ranges rather than the average. At even a conservative $200/year haircut-specific spend across ~9-10 million men, that's **roughly $1.8-2 billion/year already being spent** by exactly the audience this app serves — money currently being spent blind, with no reliable way to route it toward someone who'll actually do a good job.

The broader men's grooming products market itself is sized very differently across research firms (estimates range from roughly $60 billion to nearly $300 billion globally depending on methodology and product scope) — worth treating as a directional backdrop rather than a precise number, since market research firms in this space don't agree with each other.

## Business models, and how they'd function

1. **Subscription (barber side).** The proven model in this exact category — theCut generates revenue through a subscription model for barbers who use the platform to manage and grow their business. Applied here: barbers pay for a claimed, enhanced profile once they have enough vouches to make it worth showing off.
2. **Booking take rate**, once/if booking integration gets built — a small percentage per transaction, the way Squire and theCut both layer a transaction fee on top of subscriptions.
3. **Affiliate revenue** on hair products, riding the community's existing habit of recommending things to each other.
4. **Data licensing** — the six-city research already generates a real, sellable insight (which neighborhoods are underserved) that barbershop chains and product brands would plausibly pay for.

**Sequencing caveat:** subscriptions only work once there's enough vouch density that a barber actually wants to pay to be featured. Early on, before that density exists, there's effectively no revenue — the entire model depends on the human-outreach step (seeding real vouches city by city) discussed throughout this project being real and working.

## Valuation, if this gets heavy traction

Two real comps exist in exactly this space:

- **theCut** raised only $5.35 million total funding and bootstrapped its way to facilitating over $2 billion in haircuts booked — proof this category can generate large transaction volume on very little capital.
- **Squire** took the opposite path: a $750 million valuation on the back of a $60 million Series D round in 2021, priced at roughly a 62.5x revenue multiple on about $12M revenue at the time. This was a pandemic-era, liquidity-fueled number and should be treated as a historical outlier, not a benchmark to plan around.

Current market conditions are far more sober than 2021: vertical SaaS M&A deals are pricing at a median of 5.5x EV/revenue in 2026, with premium vertical assets reaching 7.8x, and public SaaS multiples have actually compressed to around 3.3x as of early 2026 — down sharply from 2024's 6.2x. 62.5x-style multiples are not a realistic planning assumption today.

**A rough "heavy traction" scenario:** meaningful vouch density across 20-30 real cities, a working subscription base, some booking take-rate revenue, and modest affiliate/data income — a plausible mature annual revenue figure might land somewhere in the **$8-12M/year** range. Applying the realistic 4-8x vertical-marketplace multiple above gives a valuation in the neighborhood of **$35-90M**.

**A genuinely exceptional outcome** — multi-continent adoption, the taxonomy expanding credibly beyond Asian hair, real cultural-phenomenon-level community pull — could push revenue toward $20-30M/year and, at a premium multiple, into the **$150-300M range**, roughly the order of magnitude of Squire's own early-stage valuation before its later, frothier rounds.

### The honest caveat

None of this is a forecast, and the single biggest variable in all of it — by far — is the one thing no market report can size: whether real vouch density actually materializes city by city. Every number above assumes it does. The market opportunity is real and well-documented; whether this specific product captures a meaningful slice of it depends entirely on execution on the ground, not on market size.
